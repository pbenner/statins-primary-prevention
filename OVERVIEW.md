# Statins before a first cardiovascular event

Evidence checked on 26 October 2026.

**The claim that statins help only after a heart attack is false as a general statement.** Randomized trials show that statins can prevent first cardiovascular events. The more defensible concern is that the absolute benefit can be small in people at low risk, while evidence for extending life is less striking than evidence for preventing heart attacks and strokes. Whether that benefit is worth taking a daily medicine is a separate question. [WOSCOPS](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJM199511163332001), [HOPE-3](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1600176), [USPSTF evidence review](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2022.12138)

The historical literature suggests how the claim developed: strong early survival results in people with established coronary disease; primary prevention reviews with uncertain mortality results; disagreement about treating people at low risk; and concerns about harms. This is a plausible explanation of the claim, not evidence that it is what any particular doctor meant. This investigation does not establish how many doctors believe it.

## What primary and secondary prevention mean

Primary prevention means preventing a first clinical cardiovascular event in someone without established cardiovascular disease. Secondary prevention concerns people who already have atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, including coronary disease, some strokes, or peripheral arterial disease. A previous heart attack is not required for a person to fall into the latter group. Trial definitions vary: some older studies excluded previous myocardial infarction but allowed other vascular disease. [USPSTF recommendation](https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation/statin-use-in-adults-preventive-medication), [WOSCOPS](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJM199511163332001)

Three questions must be kept separate:

1. Does treatment prevent heart attacks, strokes, or other cardiovascular events?
2. Does it reduce deaths from any cause during the study period?
3. Is the absolute benefit large enough to justify harms, inconvenience, and cost for the person considering treatment?

A positive answer to the first does not guarantee a detectable answer to the second, or settle the third.

## Where the skeptical claim comes from

### Early trials established a strong survival benefit in coronary disease

The **4S trial in 1994** randomized 4,444 people with previous myocardial infarction or angina. Over a median 5.4 years, 182 people assigned simvastatin died, versus 256 assigned placebo; mortality relative risk was 0.70, with a 95% confidence interval of 0.58–0.85. Such results made secondary prevention particularly compelling. The population included angina, so even this landmark trial was not confined to people after a heart attack. [4S](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7968073/)

**WOSCOPS followed in 1995** and showed fewer coronary events in men without a previous heart attack. However, its all-cause mortality result narrowly missed the conventional significance threshold, with P=0.051. It offers an early example of clear evidence for preventing events alongside greater uncertainty about survival. [WOSCOPS](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJM199511163332001)

### Reviews emphasized uncertainty about mortality and overall health

**Therapeutics Letter 48 in 2003**, from the University of British Columbia, acknowledged fewer heart attacks and strokes but argued that an overall health benefit had not been demonstrated, emphasizing mortality and total serious adverse events. **Letter 77 in 2010** compared conflicting reviews and questioned trial bias, incomplete harms reporting, and the inclusion of secondary prevention participants in analyses described as primary prevention. These are identifiable published sources of the skeptical position. They are evidence bulletins with an interpretive argument, not additional randomized trials. [Letter 48](https://www.ti.ubc.ca/PDF/48.pdf), [Letter 77](https://www.ti.ubc.ca/wordpress/wp-content/uploads/2011/07/77.pdf)

The **2006 Thavendiranathan meta-analysis** found fewer major coronary events, RR 0.71 (0.60–0.83), but an uncertain all-cause mortality result, RR 0.92 (0.84–1.01). It therefore already supported cardiovascular benefit while leaving the survival question unresolved. [2006 meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1001/archinte.166.21.2307)

The particularly relevant **Ray et al. review in 2010** assembled primary prevention mortality data from 11 trials and 65,229 participants. There were 2,793 deaths over an average 3.7 years. The pooled mortality RR was **0.91 (0.83–1.01)**. Removing people with established disease from mixed trials addressed a legitimate concern about applicability. However, the result remained compatible with a meaningful reduction in mortality; it did not establish an effect of zero, and it did not test whether preventing nonfatal events was worthwhile. [Ray et al.](https://doi.org/10.1001/archinternmed.2010.182)

The **2011 Cochrane review** is sometimes remembered chiefly for its caution about low-risk prescribing. In fact, it reported significantly lower all-cause mortality, RR 0.83 (0.73–0.95), while criticizing selective reporting, incomplete harms data, and some inclusion of people with cardiovascular disease. The **2013 update** reached a more favorable interpretation, reporting mortality OR 0.86 (0.79–0.94) and fewer cardiovascular events. Reading only the cautious or favorable conclusion loses the distinction between observed efficacy and confidence in broad prescribing. OR means odds ratio and is not identical to RR. [2011 review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21249663/), [2013 review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6481400/)

### The BMJ debate made low risk and net benefit central

**Abramson et al. in 2013** argued against expanding statin prescribing to people at low risk, questioning overall health benefit, mortality evidence, and harms. This is an especially direct source for the argument that preventing some cardiovascular events does not necessarily establish sufficient overall benefit to justify widespread prescribing. It was a critical analysis, not a new trial. [BMJ analysis](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.f6123)

Its claim that adverse effects occurred in about 18–20% of patients was **withdrawn in a 2014 correction**. That estimate came from an uncontrolled observational study and could not establish how many events the drug caused. The correction also fixed a claim about discontinuation. The article itself was not withdrawn, and the correction explicitly distinguished its mortality argument from the corrected adverse-effect claims. The archived 2013 PDF is the original version and must be read with this correction. [Published correction](https://www.bmj.com/content/348/bmj.g3329)

My interpretation of this history is that a qualified argument—uncertain survival benefit or insufficient net benefit for people at low risk—can be compressed into the much stronger and unsupported statement that statins work only after an event.

## What the randomized trials show

These results concern each trial's own primary cardiovascular endpoint. Endpoints differ, so the percentages are not a ranking of statins or populations. MI means myocardial infarction, or heart attack; HR means hazard ratio, a comparison of event hazards over follow-up.

| Trial | Population and follow-up | Primary cardiovascular result with statin versus control | Main interpretive limit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **WOSCOPS 1995** | 6,595 men aged 45–64, high cholesterol, no previous MI; mean 4.9 years | Nonfatal MI or coronary death: **174 versus 248**; 31% relative reduction | Predominantly primary prevention; some other vascular disease was allowed; mortality P=0.051 |
| **AFCAPS/TexCAPS 1998** | 6,605 people without clinically evident atherosclerotic CVD, average LDL and low HDL; mean 5.2 years | First acute major coronary event: **116 versus 183**; RR **0.63 (0.50–0.79)** | Endpoint included unstable angina as well as MI and sudden death |
| **ASCOT-LLA 2003** | 10,305 hypertensive people with additional risk factors; median 3.3 years | Nonfatal MI or fatal coronary disease: **100 versus 154**; HR **0.64 (0.50–0.83)** | Primary coronary prevention, with some other vascular disease allowed; stopped early; mortality result uncertain |
| **CARDS 2004** | 2,838 people with type 2 diabetes and no documented CVD; median 3.9 years | Major cardiovascular event: **83 versus 127**; 37% rate reduction | Applies to diabetes with additional risk factors; stopped early; mortality P=0.059 |
| **JUPITER 2008** | 17,802 people without CVD, LDL below 130 mg/dL and elevated inflammatory marker hsCRP; median 1.9 years | Composite cardiovascular endpoint: HR **0.56 (0.46–0.69)** | Selected population and early termination; all-cause mortality HR **0.80 (0.67–0.97)** |
| **HOPE-3 2016** | 12,705 people without CVD at intermediate risk, across 21 countries; median 5.6 years | Cardiovascular death, nonfatal MI, or nonfatal stroke: **3.7% versus 4.8%**; HR **0.76 (0.64–0.91)** | Absolute reduction approximately **1.1 percentage points**; does not establish the same benefit in very low-risk people |

Sources: [WOSCOPS](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJM199511163332001), [AFCAPS/TexCAPS](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.279.20.1615), [ASCOT-LLA](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(03)12948-0), [CARDS](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(04)16895-5), [JUPITER](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa0807646), [HOPE-3](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1600176).

In particular, AFCAPS/TexCAPS, CARDS, JUPITER, and HOPE-3 provide direct randomized evidence in people without clinical CVD. Their positive results cannot be explained away as a benefit confined to previous heart-attack patients accidentally included in a pooled analysis.

## What later reviews add

The **2012 Cholesterol Treatment Trialists' Collaboration analysis**, using individual participant data from 27 trials, examined baseline risk and previous vascular disease. In participants without previous vascular disease, mortality RR was 0.91 (0.85–0.97) per 1 mmol/L reduction in LDL. Among people with a predicted five-year major vascular event risk below 10%, the estimated reduction was about **11 major vascular events per 1,000 treated over five years**, per 1 mmol/L LDL reduction. The analysis included both statin versus control and more versus less intensive treatment comparisons. Its low-risk category is not interchangeable with a modern ten-year risk score, and its primary prevention mortality estimate should not be attributed to every low-risk subgroup separately. [CTT 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3437972/)

The **2022 USPSTF evidence review** included 22 statin versus control trials with 90,624 participants. The pooled absolute differences below concern the trials' observed follow-up, ranging from six months to six years; they are not uniform five-year predictions. Different outcomes draw on different sets of trials. [USPSTF evidence review](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2022.12138)

| Outcome | Relative risk with 95% confidence interval | Fewer outcomes per 1,000 participants |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Death from any cause | **0.92 (0.87–0.98)** | **3.5** |
| Myocardial infarction | **0.67 (0.60–0.75)** | **8.5** |
| Stroke | **0.78 (0.68–0.90)** | **3.9** |
| Composite cardiovascular outcome | **0.72 (0.64–0.81)** | **12.8** |
| Cardiovascular death | **0.91 (0.81–1.02)** | Point estimate **1.3**, statistically uncertain |

Do not add these benefits: a heart attack or stroke may already be counted in a composite. A statistically significant result for total mortality alongside an uncertain cardiovascular mortality result is possible because the analyses use different data and have different precision. The review does not establish a mortality benefit for every age or risk subgroup.

## Neutral trials and older adults

**ALLHAT-LLT in 2002** found no significant reduction in mortality or coronary events. It was an open trial of pravastatin versus usual care, rather than a blinded placebo comparison; 14% already had coronary disease. Many control participants subsequently received lipid-lowering drugs, reducing the treatment contrast. It remains a relevant neutral result, but does not provide a clean comparison of sustained statin use against no statin use. [Original trial](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.288.23.2998)

The **2017 ALLHAT analysis** of older primary prevention participants also found no clear benefit. For those aged at least 75, mortality HR was 1.34 (0.98–1.84), an uncertain estimate compatible with several possibilities. This was a post hoc subgroup analysis with limited LDL separation and treatment crossover. It does not prove that statins increase mortality in older people. [2017 analysis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5543335/)

**PROSPER in 2002** enrolled people aged 70–82 with either vascular disease or risk factors. Its overall cardiovascular endpoint improved, but the primary prevention subgroup was inconclusive. A favorable result overall and an uncertain subgroup result are not, by themselves, evidence that treatment effects differ between subgroups. The **2019 CTT review** similarly highlighted less direct evidence in people over 75 without established disease. [PROSPER](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(02)11600-X), [CTT 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6429627/)

### The 2026 STAREE update

STAREE, published online in August 2026, randomized **9,971 community-dwelling adults aged at least 70**, without CVD, diabetes, or dementia, to atorvastatin or placebo. After median 5.9 years, there were **297 versus 412** primary cardiovascular events, HR **0.70 (0.61–0.82)**. The other primary endpoint—death, dementia, or persistent physical disability—was not significantly reduced, HR **0.94 (0.84–1.05)**. [STAREE publication](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa2607314)

The official ESC release reports cardiovascular event proportions of **6.0% versus 8.3%**, an absolute difference of about 2.3 percentage points. This adds evidence for preventing first cardiovascular events in older adults, while leaving extension of disability-free survival unproven. It should not automatically be generalized to frail or institutionalized people. The local archive contains the trial's abstract and an official results release, not its complete article or supplements. [ESC results release](https://www.escardio.org/news/press/press-releases/cholesterol-lowering-medication-reduces-major-cardiovascular-events-by-30-per-cent-in-older-people-without-known-cardiovascular-disease/)

## Why absolute risk changes the decision

A similar relative benefit prevents more events when untreated risk is higher. The following is **illustrative arithmetic**, assuming a 20% relative reduction in the same endpoint over five years. These are not trial results or personal risk predictions.

| Untreated five-year risk | Treated risk under the assumption | Absolute reduction | Events prevented per 1,000 | Approximate number treated for five years to prevent one event |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1% | 0.8% | 0.2 percentage points | 2 | 500 |
| 5% | 4% | 1 percentage point | 10 | 100 |
| 10% | 8% | 2 percentage points | 20 | 50 |
| 20% | 16% | 4 percentage points | 40 | 25 |

This explains why a doctor may strongly favor treatment after an event and be more selective before one, without believing the medicine suddenly starts working after damage occurs. Actual benefit also depends on LDL reduction, treatment duration, adherence, and competing illness. The CTT analyses support broadly similar proportional vascular benefit across many risk groups; the table holds that proportional benefit constant to isolate the arithmetic. [CTT 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3437972/)

## Is this Simpson's paradox

**The main controversy does not show the reversal required for Simpson's paradox.** That paradox occurs when an association has one direction within groups but reverses after groups with different compositions are pooled. The better explanations here are different endpoints, different absolute risks, different trial selection, and different statistical precision.

For example, Ray's mortality RR **0.91 (0.83–1.01)** and the 2022 review's **0.92 (0.87–0.98)** have very similar point estimates. One crosses the conventional no-effect boundary of 1; the other does not. That is not a reversal of treatment effect. Nor does a significant result in secondary prevention and a nonsignificant result in primary prevention establish a difference between their effects. Assessing that requires comparing the effects directly, with their uncertainty. [Ray](https://doi.org/10.1001/archinternmed.2010.182), [2022 review](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2022.12138)

A Simpson reversal *could* arise in observational prescribing data because doctors preferentially treat higher-risk people. Here is an **invented example**, with equal follow-up and no actual study data:

| Risk group | Statin events / people | Statin event risk | Untreated events / people | Untreated event risk |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Lower risk | 8 / 1,000 | 0.8% | 90 / 9,000 | 1% |
| Higher risk | 720 / 9,000 | 8% | 100 / 1,000 | 10% |
| Pooled | 728 / 10,000 | **7.28%** | 190 / 10,000 | **1.90%** |

Within each risk group, treated people have 20% fewer events. Pooled crudely, they appear to fare much worse because so many more of them were high risk. Randomization and trial-stratified analysis address this problem. Pooling everyone from different trials without preserving treatment comparisons could reintroduce it. The example explains a possible bias, not the observed statin trial results.

## Harms and the remaining judgment

Statins have adverse effects, and absolute benefit matters when deciding whether to accept them. The **2022 CTT analysis of blinded trials** found a small excess of muscle pain or weakness: approximately **11 additional reports per 1,000 person-years during the first year**, with no significant excess of first reports after that year overall. Most reported muscle symptoms in statin recipients also occurred at comparable rates with placebo. That does not mean an individual patient's symptoms are imaginary or unimportant. Trial participants and follow-up may differ from routine practice. [Muscle symptoms analysis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7613583/)

The **2024 CTT diabetes analysis** found a dose-dependent increase in new diabetes diagnoses: RR **1.10** with low or moderate intensity treatment and **1.36** with high intensity treatment compared with placebo. The average excess with low or moderate intensity treatment was about **0.12 percentage points per year**. Absolute rates depend on baseline glycaemia and how intensively diabetes is tested for; the high intensity trials used different ascertainment. These safety analyses include both prevention settings. Benefits and harms should be compared over compatible time periods and with attention to their severity, rather than by subtracting unlike event counts. [Diabetes analysis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7615958/)

Evidence of fewer first cardiovascular events supports primary prevention, but does not imply that every low-risk person should take a statin. Likewise, uncertainty about longevity in a subgroup does not establish that preventing a disabling stroke has no value.

## What guidelines say

Current recommendations contradict a rule requiring a previous event. Their thresholds and calculators differ and cannot be compared as if they measured identical risk.

| Guideline | Position on treatment before a first event |
| --- | --- |
| **USPSTF 2022** | Recommends prescribing for adults 40–75 with at least one risk factor and estimated ten-year CVD risk at least 10%; selectively offers treatment at 7.5% to below 10%. Its evidence statement for starting treatment at 76 or older predates STAREE. |
| **NICE NG238** | Offers atorvastatin for primary prevention at ten-year QRISK3 of at least 10%, with room for treatment below that threshold after an informed discussion. |
| **ESC/EAS 2025 update** | Explicitly recommends or considers pharmacological LDL lowering in primary prevention according to risk category and LDL concentration after nonpharmacological measures. |
| **ACC/AHA 2026** | Uses PREVENT-ASCVD risk assessment and explicitly provides for primary prevention treatment. The March guideline also predates the August STAREE publication. |

Sources: [USPSTF](https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation/statin-use-in-adults-preventive-medication), [NICE recommendations 1.6.7–1.6.8](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng238/chapter/Recommendations), [ESC/EAS update](https://eas-society.org/publications/guidelines/2025-focused-update-of-the-2019-esc-eas-guidelines-for-the-management-of-dyslipidaemias/), [ACC/AHA official release](https://www.acc.org/About-ACC/Press-Releases/2026/03/13/18/01/ACCAHA-Issue-UpDated-Guideline-for-Managing-Lipids-Cholesterol).

## Suggested reading order

1. **Ray 2010**, then **Therapeutics Letter 77**, to understand the historical mortality and net-benefit argument.
2. **Abramson 2013 together with its 2014 correction**, to understand the low-risk prescribing controversy.
3. **HOPE-3**, to see a direct trial of primary prevention rather than relying only on pooled evidence.
4. **CTT 2012** and **the 2022 USPSTF review**, to compare relative effects, absolute benefits, and mortality estimates.
5. **ALLHAT's older subgroup** and **STAREE 2026**, to see why the endpoint and the population matter.
